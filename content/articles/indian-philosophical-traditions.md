"Indian philosophy" is often presented as a single, serene wisdom. The reality is livelier. For centuries, thinkers across the subcontinent built rival systems, wrote commentaries on commentaries and attacked one another's arguments in detail. Even the idea that there are exactly "six schools" turns out to have a history of its own.

## What is a darśana?

The Sanskrit word usually translated as "school of philosophy" is *darśana*, from a root meaning "to see". A darśana is a vision or view — a systematic way of seeing how things are.[^1] How many darśanas there are depends on how you count: Buddhist and Jain philosophy alone contain many distinct traditions.[^1]

## Āstika and nāstika

A common way of sorting the schools is by their attitude to the Vedas. Schools that recognise the authority of the Vedas are called *āstika*; those that do not are *nāstika*.[^1] These words are sometimes translated as "theist" and "atheist", but that is misleading: the line is drawn at the Vedas, not at belief in God.

Today it is usual to list six āstika schools in three pairs — Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya and Yoga, Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta — and three nāstika traditions: the Jains, the Buddhists and the materialists, known as Lokāyata or Cārvāka.[^3] Even within the āstika group, the relationship to the Vedas varied. The foundational text of Sāṃkhya, the *Sāṅkhya Kārikā*, does not explicitly mention the Vedas at all.[^1]

## How do you know?

Much Indian philosophy begins with epistemology: what are the reliable sources of knowledge, or *pramāṇas*? The schools gave different answers.

- **Nyāya** accepted four: perception, inference, analogy and the testimony of reliable persons.[^1]
- **Cārvāka** is usually described as accepting perception alone, rejecting inference and testimony as independent sources.[^2] Some accounts suggest a subtler view: everyday inferences grounded in experience — smoke, therefore fire — were acceptable, but not inferences about things no one has ever perceived, such as gods or an afterlife.[^2]

Nyāya also took the ethics of argument seriously. Its founding text distinguishes *vāda*, a properly conducted discussion aimed at the truth, from *jalpa*, debate aimed only at beating the opponent, and *vitaṇḍā*, attacking another view without defending any of your own.[^1]

## What is there?

Other schools focused on metaphysics. Vaiśeṣika described reality through six categories: substance, quality, action, universal, particularity and inherence — the relation that binds a quality to its substance.[^1]

Vedānta grew out of commentaries on the *Brahma Sūtra*. Three became especially influential: Śaṅkara's in the eighth century (Advaita, non-dualism), Rāmānuja's in the twelfth (Viśiṣṭādvaita, qualified non-dualism) and Madhva's in the thirteenth (Dvaita, dualism).[^1] They disagreed sharply about the relationship between the individual self and the ultimate reality, Brahman.

## Why "six"? A list with a history

The tidy list of six orthodox schools is a convention that developed over centuries.[^1] Older surveys of Indian thought counted differently.

- The term darśana is first used for a philosophical school in the fifth century, by the Buddhist thinker Bhāvaviveka.[^3]
- The Jain monk Haribhadra, writing around the end of the eighth century, also described six systems — but his six were the Buddhists, Nyāya, Sāṃkhya, the Jains, Vaiśeṣika and Mīmāṃsā. Yoga and Vedānta do not appear as separate schools.[^3]
- In the ninth century, the Nyāya philosopher Jayanta Bhaṭṭa discussed six schools that included the Cārvākas.[^3]
- Around 1350, Mādhava-Vidyāraṇya's *Sarvadarśanasaṅgraha* surveyed sixteen systems, beginning with the Cārvākas and ending with Śaṅkara's Vedānta, as if Advaita were the culmination of all the rest.[^3]

In other words, who counts as one of "the six" depends on who is doing the counting — and why.

:::note Hearing the materialists
No complete text of the Cārvāka school survives; only fragments remain.[^2] Most of what we know about them comes from their opponents, who had every reason to make them sound crude. Reading Indian philosophy well means remembering whose voices were preserved.
:::

## A thought to carry

Nyāya's distinction between *vāda* and *jalpa* is more than two thousand years old, and it has never been more useful. Before your next argument — online or at the dinner table — ask yourself which one you are having: a discussion to find out what is true, or a contest to win.
