Walk into a home in Mithila on the eve of a wedding and you may find women at work on a wall. They are painting the *kohbar ghar*, the room where a newly married couple will spend their first nights — and filling it with gods, goddesses, lotuses, fish and birds. What the world now calls Madhubani painting began here, as a ritual made by women for their families, long before anyone thought of it as art to sell.

## A painting with a purpose

Mithila is the old name for the plains of north Bihar and the neighbouring Tarai of Nepal. Literary references suggest that women there have painted walls and floors for domestic rituals since at least the fourteenth century.[^3] The paintings were never decoration alone. Women of the household painted the walls to consecrate the spaces where rites of passage took place — and none was more elaborate than a wedding.[^2]

The kohbar wall carries a precise symbolic language. A ring of lotus blossoms stands for the bride and a stand of bamboo for the groom; fish, snakes and turtles, creatures that produce many offspring, express hopes for the couple's family.[^2] The lotus, adopted from the ponds that bloom across Mithila in the monsoon, is an emblem of fertility and abundance.[^5] Alongside the wall paintings, married women draw *aripan*, auspicious ritual designs, on the floor.[^2]

## An earthquake and an Englishman

On 15 January 1934 a devastating earthquake struck north Bihar. William Archer, a British civil servant posted in the area, noticed that the walls of ruined houses revealed elaborate paintings in what had been inner rooms.[^1] He went on to study and photograph them, and in 1949 published the first analysis of what he called Maithil painting in the art magazine *Marg*.[^2]

It is worth being careful with the word "discovered". The paintings had been made, seen and understood by Maithil families for centuries; what changed in 1934 was that an outsider with influence began to write about them. Archer's account also shaped which painters were taken seriously. Because most of the homes he first saw belonged to Brahmin and Kayastha families, he stressed those communities — a framing that, scholars argue, long kept other painters out of view.[^1] [^3]

> Inescapable elements of strangeness, the sense of wonder, a mystery burning at the heart of life.
> — W. G. Archer on the paintings, writing in *Marg* (1949), as quoted by Sarmaya[^1]

## From walls to paper

The turning point came with hardship. During the drought of the mid-1960s, Pupul Jayakar, who led the All India Handicrafts Board, sent the artist Bhaskar Kulkarni to Mithila in 1966 to look for the paintings.[^1] In villages such as Jitwarpur and Ranti, some women agreed to recreate their wall images on paper. Their work went on sale at the new government emporia in New Delhi, which opened in 1967, as part of a relief effort.[^1] [^2]

The move to paper changed lives. Painting, once unpaid household work, became an income — and the painters became known by name. Ganga Devi and Mahasundari Devi, from Kayastha families, and Sita Devi and Jagdamba Devi, from Brahmin families, quickly gained acclaim. Sita Devi and Ganga Devi travelled abroad in the 1970s, and new places and experiences began to appear in their work.[^1]

## Many hands, many styles

Mithila painting is not one style. Painters from Kayastha families were associated with fine line work in black and red, while Brahmin painters were known for brilliant colour.[^6] These associations are real but political, and they have shifted as techniques passed between neighbours.[^3]

Some of the most important innovations came from Dalit painters. Jamuna Devi, from the Chamar community, pioneered the light brown cow-dung wash that many painters, upper-caste ones included, now use to make paper look like a mud-plastered wall. She and Shanti Devi, from the Dusadh community, also drew on the techniques of their eminent Brahmin neighbour, Sita Devi.[^3] Dusadh artists have long painted episodes from the oral epic of their culture hero, Raja Salhesh — a way, the anthropologist David Szanton argues, of placing their own stories alongside the Ramayana.[^3] In 1987, Jamuna Devi became the first celebrated Mithila painter from the Chamar community to receive a national handicrafts award.[^2]

:::note How to buy Madhubani well
Ask for the artist's name and village, and whether the work is an original painting or a print. Prices for originals reflect days of work. Buying directly from artists, artist collectives or institutions that name their painters keeps more of the money with the people who make the work.
:::

## Recognition, and what it leaves out

National honours followed. Jagdamba Devi received the National Award for Master Craftsmen in 1970, followed by Sita Devi in 1975 and Ganga Devi in 1976; by the late 1970s and 1980s, Mithila painters were receiving the Padma Shri.[^2] In 2003 the Ethnic Arts Foundation founded the Mithila Art Institute in Madhubani, a free art school taught by local painters.[^3] In 2007 "Madhubani Paintings" was registered as a Geographical Indication for Bihar — legal recognition that the name belongs to a place and its makers.[^4]

{{figure madhubani-artist-dilli-haat "A Mithila painter at work at Dilli Haat, New Delhi."}}

Recognition has its own questions. The scholar Paula Richman notes that Mithila painting has been labelled ritual art, folk art, tourist art and handicraft, but rarely "modern" art — and that each label has been used to praise, constrain or appropriate it.[^2] Painters today take on climate change, a tsunami and city life as readily as gods and goddesses.[^2] To call the tradition "folk" and stop there is to miss what its artists are doing now.

## A thought to carry

A kohbar painting was made for one couple, one room and one moment. That it now hangs in museums does not cancel its first meaning; it adds another. The next time you see Madhubani on a wall, a saree or a mug, ask whose hands made it — and whether they were paid for it.
