A plate is a map: how geography shapes what India eats

There is no such thing as "Indian food". There are hundreds of regional food cultures, each shaped by rain, rivers, trade and memory.

The Cultured Human editors14 November 20263 min read

Ask ten people in India what they ate for lunch and you may get ten different maps of the country. Rice or wheat or millet; mustard oil, coconut oil or ghee; fish, mutton or no meat at all. Regional food in India is a record of geography and history — of how much rain falls, which rivers run nearby, who traded along the coast and who arrived from elsewhere.

Grain follows the rain

The first thing a plate tells you is how wet the land is. Rice needs water, and it dominates in the river deltas and the rain-soaked south and east. In drier regions, hardier grains take its place. Millets can grow on arid land with few inputs and cope well with a changing climate.1 That resilience is one reason India proposed — with the support of 72 countries — that the United Nations name 2023 the International Year of Millets.1 2

The feast on a leaf

In Kerala, the grandest meal is the sadya, a vegetarian feast of rice with many accompaniments, savouries, pickles and desserts, served on a plantain leaf. Tradition is precise: the tapering end of the leaf points to the guest's left, and the rice is served on the lower half. The sadya served on Thiruvonam, the most auspicious day of Onam, is the Onasadya.3

Bihar's portable meal

Litti-chokha is the taste of Bihar: balls of wheat dough stuffed with sattu, roasted gram flour, baked over a fire and eaten with chokha, a mash of roasted aubergine, potato or tomato. It is a meal that needs little water and travels well. In 2025 a producers' group from Ara in Bhojpur district applied for a Geographical Indication for Litti Chokha; at the time of writing, the application is still under examination.4

Bihar already has food GIs. Mithila Makhana — fox nut, harvested from ponds across north Bihar — was registered as a Geographical Indication in 2022.5

Rivers, ponds and mustard

Travel east and the rivers take over. In Bengal, freshwater fish cooked in a sharp mustard sauce is a signature of the home kitchen. Further north-east, an Assamese thali sets rice at the centre of small bowls of dal, greens, vegetables and fish.

The feast of the waza

In Kashmir, the grand formal banquet is the wazwan, prepared by hereditary cooks known as waza. It is a multi-course, mostly meat-based meal; guests traditionally sit in groups of four and share from a large copper plate called the traem.6

The newcomers

Regional food is not frozen in time. Some of the most "Indian" ingredients arrived only a few centuries ago. Chilli comes from the Americas; the food historian K. T. Achaya believed it probably reached India with the Portuguese, and it spread because, unlike black pepper, it could be grown almost anywhere.7

There is no mention whatsoever of the chilli in Indian literature before the 16th century.

— K. T. Achaya, as quoted in the Hindustan Times8

The earliest Indian reference to chillies is often traced to a Kannada poem by Purandara Dasa (1480–1564). Yet as late as 1590, none of the fifty-odd recipes in the Ain-i-Akbari, the chronicle of Akbar's court, used anything but black pepper for heat.9

Eat like a guest

When you travel, eat what is local and in season, ask how a dish is made and who makes it, and try the home-style food alongside the restaurant classics. The best regional cooking is often found in someone's kitchen, not on a menu.

A thought to carry

Every regional dish is an answer to a question: what grows here, what keeps, what the community can share. Before you call any dish "authentic", ask which place, which family and which century you mean.
